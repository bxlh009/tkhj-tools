The transition to the new TOEFL iBT format has replaced the traditional independent and integrated writing essays with a single, condensed task: the Academic Discussion. For many test-takers, this shift is not merely a reduction in word count but a fundamental change in cognitive load and strategic approach. The most common reason learners remain stuck at a score of 22–23 in the Writing section is not a lack of vocabulary or grammar, but an inability to make rapid, high-value decisions under time pressure.

To move from a 22 to a 25+, you must stop treating this task as an essay and start treating it as a strategic contribution to a conversation. This article provides a repeatable method for analyzing the prompt, structuring your response, and executing your argument within the strict constraints of the new format.

## The Core Decision: Contribution vs. Creation

The primary decision you must make before typing a single word is whether you are **creating** a new argument or **contributing** to an existing one.

In the old Independent Writing task, you had 30 minutes to generate a thesis, support it with two distinct examples, and conclude. In the Academic Discussion task, you have 10 minutes to read a professor’s prompt, read two student responses (one agreeing, one disagreeing or offering a nuance), and write a post that adds to the discussion.

If you attempt to write a standalone essay, you will fail to meet the "development" criteria because your points will feel disconnected from the specific context provided. If you simply agree with one student without adding new information, you will fail the "language use" and "topic development" criteria because your response lacks sufficient depth.

**The Decision Rule:** Your response must explicitly acknowledge the existing discussion while introducing a distinct, well-supported perspective that advances the conversation. You are not writing an essay; you are writing a high-quality forum post that demonstrates academic literacy.

## The Repeatable Method: The 10-Minute Protocol

Success in this task relies on a rigid time-management structure. Deviating from this structure leads to incomplete thoughts or grammatical errors in the final sentences. The following protocol is designed to maximize your score potential by ensuring all three scoring dimensions—Topic Development, Language Use, and Language Form—are addressed efficiently.

### Phase 1: Analysis (Minutes 0–2)

Do not start writing immediately. Spend the first two minutes dissecting the prompt.

1.  **Identify the Professor’s Question:** What is the core concept being debated? Is it about methodology, ethics, historical interpretation, or practical application?
2.  **Analyze Student A and Student B:** Note their positions. Do they agree? Do they disagree? Do they offer different evidence?
3.  **Find the Gap:** Where is the missing piece? Often, Student A focuses on theory, while Student B focuses on practice. Your contribution might bridge these two, or introduce a third dimension (e.g., economic impact, long-term sustainability, or ethical implications).

**Why this works:** ETS raters look for "relevant and well-developed" ideas. By identifying the gap, you ensure your contribution is relevant to the specific discussion, not just a generic response to the professor’s initial question.

### Phase 2: Structuring (Minutes 2–4)

Draft a mental or quick written outline using the **Point-Explanation-Evidence-Link (PEEL)** structure, adapted for brevity.

*   **Point:** State your position clearly in the first sentence.
*   **Explanation:** Clarify why this position is valid.
*   **Evidence/Example:** Provide a specific, concrete example. This can be personal, historical, or hypothetical, but it must be detailed.
*   **Link:** Connect back to the main discussion or the professor’s question.

**Constraint Check:** Your total word count should aim for 100–120 words. Anything less risks insufficient development; anything more risks running out of time and making careless errors.

### Phase 3: Execution (Minutes 4–9)

Write your response. Focus on clarity over complexity. Use varied sentence structures, but prioritize accuracy. Ensure you use transition words to show how your point relates to the previous students’ comments (e.g., "While Student A correctly notes X, I would add Y...").

### Phase 4: Review (Minutes 9–10)

Spend the final minute checking for:
*   Subject-verb agreement.
*   Article usage (a/an/the).
*   Punctuation.
*   Spelling of key terms.

**Why this works:** Minor grammatical errors do not lower your score significantly if the meaning is clear. However, incomplete sentences or missing punctuation at the end of a paragraph signal poor control, which impacts the "Language Form" criterion.

## Original Practice Example 1: Environmental Science

**Prompt Context:**
Professor Harrison asks: *How can urban cities reduce their carbon footprint effectively?*

**Student 1 (Maya):** *I believe the most effective way is to invest heavily in public transportation systems. When people have reliable, affordable buses and trains, they are less likely to drive private cars, which directly reduces emissions.*

**Student 2 (Leo):** *I disagree that transportation is the priority. Residential energy consumption is a huge factor. Cities should mandate solar panels on all new buildings and offer tax incentives for retrofitting older homes with better insulation.*

**Your Task:** Write a response to the professor’s question that contributes to the discussion.

### Analysis of the Decision Point

Maya focuses on **transportation infrastructure**. Leo focuses on **residential energy efficiency**. Both are valid, but they address different sectors. A safe response might argue for one over the other, but a higher-scoring response integrates them or introduces a third critical sector: **waste management** or **urban planning density**.

Let us choose to contribute by focusing on **urban planning density**, arguing that compact cities naturally reduce both transportation needs and energy consumption.

### Original Practice Example 1: Model Response

> While both Maya and Leo highlight important sectors, I believe urban planning density offers a more holistic solution. Compact city designs reduce the need for extensive infrastructure, thereby lowering both transportation emissions and residential energy demands. For example, in cities like Copenhagen, high-density housing is paired with pedestrian-friendly zones. Residents walk or cycle to work, reducing car dependency, while shared walls in apartment complexes improve thermal efficiency, lowering heating costs. This integrated approach addresses the root cause of consumption rather than treating symptoms separately. Therefore, prioritizing dense, mixed-use developments may yield greater long-term carbon reductions than isolated improvements in transport or housing alone.

### Evidence-Based Reasoning for the Model Response

1.  **Relevance:** The response directly addresses the professor’s question about reducing carbon footprints.
2.  **Contribution:** It does not merely repeat Maya or Leo. It synthesizes their concerns (transport and energy) into a broader structural argument (density). This demonstrates "advanced" topic development.
3.  **Specificity:** The mention of Copenhagen and the explanation of "shared walls" providing thermal efficiency provides concrete evidence. Generic statements like "cities should be smaller" would score lower.
4.  **Structure:** The response follows the PEEL structure:
    *   **Point:** Urban planning density is holistic.
    *   **Explanation:** Compact designs reduce infrastructure needs.
    *   **Evidence:** Copenhagen example with specific mechanisms (walking/cycling, shared walls).
    *   **Link:** Concludes that this approach is superior to isolated improvements.
5.  **Language Use:** Uses complex sentence structures ("While both Maya and Leo...") and precise vocabulary ("holistic," "thermal efficiency," "mixed-use developments").

### Common Misapplication in Example 1

A low-scoring response might say: *"I agree with Maya. Public transport is good. Cars cause pollution. We should build more trains."*

This fails because:
*   It lacks development (only one sentence of reasoning).
*   It ignores Leo’s point entirely, showing a lack of engagement with the full discussion.
*   The example is generic and lacks detail.

## Original Practice Example 2: History and Education

**Prompt Context:**
Professor Chen asks: *Should schools teach history through primary sources or secondary summaries?*

**Student 1 (Sam):** *Schools should focus on primary sources. Analyzing original documents helps students develop critical thinking skills and understand historical bias. It makes history feel real and engaging.*

**Student 2 (Jenna):** *Primary sources can be difficult for younger students to understand. Secondary summaries provide a clear narrative framework. Students need to grasp the big picture before they can analyze individual documents effectively.*

**Your Task:** Write a response that contributes to the discussion.

### Analysis of the Decision Point

Sam argues for **skill development** (critical thinking) via **primary sources**. Jenna argues for **cognitive accessibility** (narrative framework) via **secondary sources**. The tension is between depth of analysis and breadth of understanding.

A strong contribution might propose a **sequential approach**: using secondary sources first to build context, then primary sources for deeper analysis. This resolves the conflict by validating both points.

### Original Practice Example 2: Model Response

> I suggest a sequential approach that leverages the strengths of both methods. Secondary summaries should be used initially to establish a chronological framework and basic facts, ensuring all students share a common baseline of knowledge. Once this foundation is set, teachers can introduce primary sources to challenge students’ understanding. For instance, after learning about the Industrial Revolution from a textbook, students could analyze factory owner diaries versus worker letters. This contrast reveals differing perspectives and biases, allowing students to apply critical thinking skills to a context they already understand. This method ensures accessibility while still fostering analytical depth.

### Evidence-Based Reasoning for the Model Response

1.  **Synthesis:** The response acknowledges the validity of both Sam and Jenna. It does not dismiss either view but combines them into a pedagogical strategy.
2.  **Development:** The explanation of *why* this works (baseline knowledge -> application of critical thinking) is logically sound.
3.  **Concrete Example:** The Industrial Revolution example is specific. It names the type of primary sources (diaries vs. letters) and explains the outcome (revealing differing perspectives).
4.  **Clarity:** The language is direct and academic. The transition "Once this foundation is set" clearly marks the shift in the teaching process.
5.  **Word Count:** The response is approximately 100 words, fitting the ideal range for depth without risking time exhaustion.

### Common Misapplication in Example 2

A low-scoring response might say: *"History is boring. Teachers should make it fun. Primary sources are old papers. Secondary sources are books. I think primary sources are better because they are real."*

This fails because:
*   The tone is informal and inappropriate for an academic discussion.
*   The reasoning is circular and superficial ("because they are real").
*   It ignores the pedagogical argument made by Jenna about student comprehension levels.

## Boundaries and Limitations of This Method

While the 10-minute protocol is highly effective, it has limitations that test-takers must recognize.

### 1. The "Generic Example" Trap
Raters can easily identify vague examples. Phrases like "For example, many people believe..." or "In today's world..." are red flags. Your examples must be specific. Instead of saying "Pollution is bad," describe a specific incident or a measurable trend. If you are unsure of a factual historical event, create a plausible hypothetical scenario that illustrates the principle, but ensure it is clearly framed as an illustrative example.

### 2. Over-Complication of Vocabulary
Using complex words incorrectly is worse than using simple words correctly. If you are not confident in the usage of words like "ubiquitous," "paradigm," or "juxtaposition," avoid them. Clarity is the primary goal. A simple sentence with perfect grammar and a strong logical link will score higher than a complex sentence with multiple errors.

### 3. Ignoring the Other Students
A common mistake is to treat the two student posts as background noise. They are not. They are part of the prompt. Your response must explicitly reference them. Use phrases like "As Student A noted..." or "Contrary to Student B's claim..." This proves you have read and processed the entire discussion, which is a key component of the "Topic Development" criterion.

### 4. Time Management Rigidity
The 2-2-4-2 minute split is a guideline, not a law. If you find yourself struggling to find a unique angle in the first two minutes, spend an extra minute analyzing. However, never sacrifice the review phase. Leaving a response with obvious spelling errors or missing punctuation is a preventable error that lowers your score.

## Conclusion

Breaking through the score plateau of 22 requires a shift in mindset. You are no longer writing an essay; you are participating in an academic dialogue. By making the deliberate choice to contribute a distinct, well-supported perspective rather than simply restating an opinion, and by adhering to a strict time-management protocol, you can consistently produce high-scoring responses.

Remember: Relevance, Specificity, and Synthesis are your three pillars. Focus on these elements, and your writing score will reflect the depth of your academic engagement.
