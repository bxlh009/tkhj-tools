## The Exact Decision You Must Make on TOEFL Speaking Task 1

TOEFL Speaking Task 1 asks you to express and defend an opinion on a familiar topic. You have 15 seconds to prepare and 45 seconds to speak. The single decision that determines your score is not which opinion you choose—it is whether you commit to a clear position and sustain it throughout the response.

ETS designs this task to measure your ability to produce extended spoken discourse on a familiar topic (ETS, n.d.-a). The scoring rubric rewards a well-organized response in which the speaker develops a position with relevant reasons and examples. A response that hedges, flips positions mid-answer, or fails to take a stance cannot earn a high score, regardless of vocabulary or grammar.

This article gives you a repeatable method for making that commitment quickly and defending it effectively. It includes two original practice examples with full answers and reasoning.

## What Task 1 Actually Measures

TOEFL iBT Speaking Task 1 is the independent speaking task. ETS describes it as requiring test takers to speak about personal opinions or experiences on familiar topics (ETS, n.d.-a). There is no reading passage and no listening passage to reference. You rely entirely on your own knowledge and reasoning.

The task format is fixed:

- **Preparation time:** 15 seconds
- **Response time:** 45 seconds
- **Prompt type:** Opinion-based, preference-based, or agreement/disagreement

The scoring focuses on three dimensions:

1. **Delivery** — clarity, pace, and pronunciation
2. **Language use** — grammatical accuracy and vocabulary range
3. **Topic development** — coherence, progression of ideas, and use of supporting details

Topic development is where most learners lose points. A scattered response with weak examples will score lower than a simpler response with a clear structure and concrete support. The method below targets topic development directly.

## A Repeatable Four-Step Method

### Step 1: Identify the prompt type and commit to a position (0–5 seconds)

Read the prompt carefully. Determine whether it asks for a preference, an agreement/disagreement, or a choice between two options. Then state your position in a single sentence.

**Why this works.** The 15-second preparation window is too short for open-ended brainstorming. A quick classification lets you activate a mental template. Committing to a position early prevents mid-response hedging, which is the most common cause of low topic-development scores.

**What to do:** Mentally label the prompt type and produce one sentence such as "I believe that…" or "I prefer…" Write this sentence at the top of your notes if you choose to take notes during preparation.

### Step 2: Generate two supporting points with specific details (5–12 seconds)

For your chosen position, identify two distinct reasons. For each reason, attach one concrete detail—a specific example, a brief scenario, or a personal observation.

**Why this works.** ETS scoring criteria require "relevant elaboration" and "specific details" (ETS, n.d.-b). Two points with details fit comfortably within 45 seconds. One point with extensive detail is riskier because you may run out of time before developing it fully. Three points with no detail sound list-like and superficial.

**What to do:** Use a simple note format:

```
Position: I believe…
Point 1: Reason — Detail
Point 2: Reason — Detail
```

Keep each line to 3–6 words. You are not writing an essay; you are creating a speaking outline.

### Step 3: Structure the response using a three-part frame (12–15 seconds)

Organize your notes into a clear structure:

- **Opening:** State your position in one sentence.
- **Body:** Deliver Point 1 with its detail, then Point 2 with its detail.
- **Closing:** One sentence that reinforces your position or summarizes the two points.

**Why this works.** A three-part frame gives the listener (and the rater) an immediate signal that you understand discourse organization. The opening establishes direction. The body provides development. The closing provides closure. Raters use this structural clarity as evidence of coherent progression.

**What to do:** During the final seconds of preparation, mentally rehearse the opening sentence and the transition words you will use ("First," "Second," "For example," "In conclusion").

### Step 4: Deliver with steady pacing and natural transitions (0–45 seconds)

Speak at a moderate pace. Use the transitions you planned. If you lose your place, return to your notes and continue from the next point. Do not restart from the beginning unless you have significant time remaining.

**Why this works.** Pacing affects both delivery and topic development scores. Speaking too quickly reduces clarity and leaves less time for elaboration. Pausing briefly between points gives the rater time to register structure. Natural transitions ("The first reason is…", "Another important factor is…") signal organization without sounding mechanical.

**What to do:** Aim for approximately 100–120 words per minute. This pace allows you to fit a complete response within 45 seconds without rushing.

## Original practice example 1

**Prompt (non-official):** Some universities require all students to wear uniforms. Do you think this is a good idea? Use specific reasons and examples to support your response.

### Applying the method

**Step 1 — Position:** I believe that requiring uniforms is a good idea.

**Step 2 — Points with details:**

```
Position: Uniforms are beneficial.
Point 1: Reduces social pressure — Students judge each other less by clothing brands.
Point 2: Saves time — No need to choose outfits each morning.
```

**Step 3 — Structure:**

- Opening: I believe that requiring uniforms is a good idea.
- Body: First, uniforms reduce social pressure because students judge each other less by clothing brands. For example, at my high school, students who wore expensive brands were often treated differently, but after the uniform policy started, those differences disappeared. Second, uniforms save time because students do not need to choose outfits each morning. A student can simply put on the uniform and leave, which is especially helpful during busy exam weeks.
- Closing: For these reasons, I think uniforms are a positive requirement.

### Full sample response

I believe that requiring uniforms is a good idea. First, uniforms reduce social pressure because students judge each other less by clothing brands. For example, at my high school, students who wore expensive brands were often treated differently, but after the uniform policy started, those differences disappeared. Second, uniforms save time because students do not need to choose outfits each morning. A student can simply put on the uniform and leave, which is especially helpful during busy exam weeks. For these reasons, I think uniforms are a positive requirement.

### Why this response works

The response follows the three-part frame exactly. The opening states a clear position. Each body sentence contains a reason followed by a specific detail. The detail in Point 1 references a concrete school experience. The detail in Point 2 references a realistic student situation (exam weeks). The closing reinforces the position without introducing new information. The word count is approximately 95 words, which fits comfortably within 45 seconds at a moderate pace.

## Original practice example 2

**Prompt (non-official):** Do you agree or disagree with the following statement? Technology has improved education more than it has harmed it. Use specific reasons and examples to support your response.

### Applying the method

**Step 1 — Position:** I agree that technology has improved education more than it has harmed it.

**Step 2 — Points with details:**

```
Position: Technology has improved education.
Point 1: Increases access — Online courses allow remote students to learn.
Point 2: Enhances engagement — Interactive tools make lessons more effective.
```

**Step 3 — Structure:**

- Opening: I agree that technology has improved education more than it has harmed it.
- Body: First, technology increases access to education because online courses allow students in remote areas to learn from qualified teachers. For instance, a student living in a rural village can now enroll in a university course through a digital platform, which was impossible twenty years ago. Second, technology enhances engagement because interactive tools make lessons more effective. In my own experience, using educational apps to practice vocabulary improved my retention significantly compared to traditional memorization methods.
- Closing: Although technology has some drawbacks, its benefits for access and engagement make it a net positive for education.

### Full sample response

I agree that technology has improved education more than it has harmed it. First, technology increases access to education because online courses allow students in remote areas to learn from qualified teachers. For instance, a student living in a rural village can now enroll in a university course through a digital platform, which was impossible twenty years ago. Second, technology enhances engagement because interactive tools make lessons more effective. In my own experience, using educational apps to practice vocabulary improved my retention significantly compared to traditional memorization methods. Although technology has some drawbacks, its benefits for access and engagement make it a net positive for education.

### Why this response works

This response demonstrates a more nuanced position. The opening states agreement clearly. The body provides two distinct reasons, each supported by a specific example. The example in Point 1 references a realistic scenario (rural student accessing online courses). The example in Point 2 references a personal learning experience with a concrete comparison (apps versus traditional memorization). The closing acknowledges a counterpoint briefly before reaffirming the position, which strengthens topic development by showing the speaker can handle complexity without losing focus. The word count is approximately 110 words, which fits within 45 seconds at a moderate pace.

## Common Misapplications and Their Limits

### Hedging instead of committing

Some learners write or speak responses that avoid taking a clear position. They may say "There are pros and cons" without choosing a side. This approach fails because the task explicitly asks for an opinion. A rater cannot award high topic-development scores to a response that does not develop a position.

**Limit:** You do not need to hold a deeply held belief. The task measures your ability to organize and defend an opinion, not the moral correctness of the opinion. Choose the side that is easier to support with specific details.

### Overloading with one point

Some learners identify only one reason and attempt to develop it extensively. This often leads to running out of time before reaching a conclusion, or producing a response that sounds like a single extended example rather than a structured argument.

**Limit:** Two points with moderate development score higher than one point with exhaustive development. The 45-second limit is the constraint. Two points demonstrate breadth; one point demonstrates depth but risks incompleteness.

### Using generic examples

Phrases like "Many people believe…" or "Studies show that…" without specific details are weak supports. They do not satisfy the rubric requirement for relevant elaboration.

**Limit:** Specificity does not require personal experience. You can create plausible, detailed scenarios that illustrate your point. The key is that the detail must be concrete enough to demonstrate development, not a vague generalization.

### Speaking too fast to fit more content

Some learners rush through their response in an attempt to include additional points or examples. This reduces clarity and pronunciation quality, which lowers the delivery score. It also often results in mumbled transitions and unclear structure.

**Limit:** A well-paced response with two developed points scores higher than a rushed response with three underdeveloped points. Quality of delivery and organization matters more than quantity of content.

### Ignoring the closing sentence

Some learners finish speaking immediately after their second point, leaving the response without closure. While not always penalized heavily, a missing closing can make the response feel incomplete and may affect the coherence score.

**Limit:** The closing does not need to be elaborate. One sentence that restates the position or summarizes the two points is sufficient.

## How to Practice This Method

1. **Timed drills.** Use a timer for 15 seconds of preparation and 45 seconds of speaking. Record yourself and review the recording for structure, pacing, and clarity.
2. **Note-taking practice.** Practice writing your outline during the 15-second preparation window. Your notes should contain only keywords and short phrases, not full sentences.
3. **Variety of prompts.** Practice with prompts covering education, work, technology, health, and social issues. The method works across topics; the skill is in applying it quickly.
4. **Self-assessment.** After each practice response, evaluate whether you (a) stated a clear position, (b) provided two points with specific details, (c) used a three-part structure, and (d) spoke at a moderate pace.

## Summary

TOEFL Speaking Task 1 requires a single critical decision: commit to a position and defend it. The four-step method—classify and commit, generate two points with details, structure with a three-part frame, and deliver at a steady pace—gives you a repeatable process for making that decision and executing it under time pressure. The two original practice examples demonstrate how the method produces complete, well-organized responses within the 45-second limit. Avoid hedging, overloading, generic examples, and rushed delivery. Practice the method until it becomes automatic, and you will approach every Task 1 prompt with a clear plan.
