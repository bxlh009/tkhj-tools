## The Decision You Must Make on Every TOEFL Reading Question

When you sit for the TOEFL Reading section, each question presents a single, recurring decision: **which answer choice is directly supported by the passage text?** The test does not reward outside knowledge, personal opinion, or logical leaps that go beyond what is written. It rewards the ability to match an answer to explicit evidence.

This decision is the bottleneck for many test-takers. You may understand the passage, recognize the question type, and eliminate obviously wrong options—yet still choose incorrectly. The gap is usually not comprehension; it is the habit of selecting the answer that *feels* right rather than the answer that *proves* right.

This guide gives you a repeatable method for evidence checking. You will learn how to locate the relevant text, evaluate each choice against it, and eliminate unsupported options. You will also practice the method with original, non-official examples and see where it commonly fails.

## Why Evidence Checking Works

The TOEFL Reading section is designed to measure your ability to understand academic texts in English. Every question is anchored in the passage. Even questions that ask about inference, purpose, or vocabulary require you to ground your response in what the passage says or implies.

ETS states that the TOEFL iBT Reading section “measures your ability to understand university-level academic texts in English” and that questions “are based on the reading passages” ([ETS TOEFL Content](https://www.ets.org/toefl/test-takers/ibt/about/content.html)). Preparation materials emphasize that success depends on “reading carefully” and “answering based on the passage” ([ETS Prepare](https://www.ets.org/toefl/test-takers/ibt/prepare.html)).

In practice, this means:

- **Correct answers are paraphrases or direct restatements** of passage content.
- **Incorrect answers contain distortions**: they may be too broad, too narrow, contradicted by the passage, or based on information not present.
- **Your job is not to decide what is true in the world**, but to decide what the passage supports.

Evidence checking turns this principle into a step-by-step routine.

## The Evidence-Check Method

Follow these six steps for every Reading question. Each step is designed to keep your attention on the text and away from assumptions.

### Step 1: Identify the Question Type and What It Asks

Before you look at the answers, know what the question wants. Common types include:

- **Factual information** – “According to paragraph 2, what caused…?”
- **Negative factual** – “All of the following are mentioned EXCEPT…”
- **Inference** – “It can be inferred that…”
- **Vocabulary** – “The word ‘X’ is closest in meaning to…”
- **Purpose** – “Why does the author mention…?”
- **Sentence insertion** – “Where would the sentence best fit?”
- **Prose summary / Table completion** – “Choose the three answers that…”

Each type has a different evidence requirement. Factual questions demand direct matches. Inference questions allow a small logical step, but still require passage support. Vocabulary questions require context clues. Purpose questions require understanding the author’s intent.

**Why this step works:** It focuses your search. You know what kind of evidence to look for before you scan the passage.

### Step 2: Locate the Relevant Passage Section

Use keywords from the question to find the exact sentences you need. Look for:

- Proper nouns, dates, technical terms
- Names of people, places, theories
- Reference words (“this process,” “such organisms”)

If the question refers to a specific paragraph, stay within that paragraph. If it is a global question, scan the entire passage but note which section holds the key evidence.

**Why this step works:** It prevents you from reading the whole passage again and wasting time. It also reduces the chance of mixing evidence from different parts.

### Step 3: Read the Passage Sentences Carefully

Once you have located the relevant text, read it slowly. Pay attention to:

- Qualifiers: “some,” “all,” “often,” “never,” “may,” “must”
- Cause-and-effect relationships
- Comparisons and contrasts
- Examples and explanations

Do not rush. Misreading a single qualifier can lead you to choose a wrong answer.

**Why this step works:** It ensures you capture the exact meaning the author intended. TOEFL questions often hinge on subtle distinctions.

### Step 4: Evaluate Each Answer Choice Against the Passage

Take each option and ask: **Does the passage say this, or something very close to it?**

Check for:

- **Direct paraphrase** – The answer rewords the passage without changing meaning.
- **Supported inference** – The answer is a logical conclusion from the passage, but not explicitly stated.
- **Unsupported claim** – The answer introduces information not in the passage.
- **Contradiction** – The answer conflicts with the passage.
- **Partial match** – The answer contains some correct words but distorts the meaning.

**Why this step works:** It forces you to compare each choice to the text, rather than relying on memory or intuition.

### Step 5: Eliminate Unsupported Choices

Cross out any answer that:

- Is not mentioned in the passage
- Goes beyond what the passage supports
- Contradicts the passage
- Is too broad or too narrow compared to the passage
- Mixes correct details with incorrect ones

For negative factual questions (“All EXCEPT”), eliminate the three choices that are supported; the remaining one is correct.

**Why this step works:** It reduces the number of options and increases your confidence in the remaining choice.

### Step 6: Select the Best-Supported Answer

Choose the answer that matches the passage most closely. If two answers seem plausible, ask which one is **more directly supported**. The correct answer may not be perfect wording, but it must be defensible based on the text.

**Why this step works:** It ensures you pick the answer that aligns with the test’s design, not your personal interpretation.

## How to Prove an Answer Is Correct

You can verify a correct answer by pointing to the exact passage text that supports it. If you cannot cite a sentence or phrase, the answer is likely wrong.

For example, if a question asks about a cause, the correct answer should correspond to a sentence that states or implies that cause. If the passage says “The decline was due to habitat loss,” and an answer says “Habitat loss caused the decline,” that is a valid paraphrase.

If an answer says “Habitat loss and pollution caused the decline,” but the passage only mentions habitat loss, the answer is unsupported because it adds information.

## How to Eliminate Unsupported Answer Choices

Common traps include:

- **Extreme language** – Words like “all,” “never,” “always” are often wrong unless the passage uses them.
- **Out-of-scope information** – Answers that bring in outside facts or general knowledge.
- **Reverse logic** – Answers that swap cause and effect or subject and object.
- **Partial truth** – Answers that contain a correct detail but attach it to the wrong context.
- **Misplaced emphasis** – Answers that focus on a minor detail while missing the main point.

When you see these traps, eliminate the choice. Do not try to justify it.

## Original Practice Example 1

**Passage (non-official):**

The development of urban green spaces has been linked to improved mental health outcomes. Studies show that residents who live near parks and gardens report lower levels of stress and anxiety. The presence of vegetation can reduce exposure to noise pollution and improve air quality, both of which contribute to well-being. Additionally, green spaces encourage physical activity and social interaction, further supporting mental health. However, access to these spaces is not evenly distributed; lower-income neighborhoods often have fewer parks, creating disparities in health outcomes.

**Question:**

According to the passage, what is one reason that green spaces improve mental health?

**Answer choices:**

A. They reduce noise pollution and improve air quality.
B. They eliminate stress and anxiety completely.
C. They are evenly distributed across all neighborhoods.
D. They encourage people to move to rural areas.

**Evidence-based reasoning:**

- **Choice A** is supported by the passage: “The presence of vegetation can reduce exposure to noise pollution and improve air quality, both of which contribute to well-being.” This directly matches the answer.
- **Choice B** is unsupported: the passage says green spaces are “linked to improved mental health” and “report lower levels of stress and anxiety,” not that they eliminate these conditions.
- **Choice C** is contradicted: the passage states “access to these spaces is not evenly distributed.”
- **Choice D** is out of scope: the passage does not mention rural migration.

**Correct answer: A**

## Original Practice Example 2

**Passage (non-official):**

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the late 18th century and transformed manufacturing. Before this period, most goods were produced by hand in homes or small workshops. The invention of machines such as the spinning jenny and the power loom allowed for mass production of textiles. Factories emerged, concentrating workers and machinery in one location. This shift increased output dramatically but also led to poor working conditions, long hours, and child labor. Over time, reforms were introduced to improve safety and limit child employment.

**Question:**

What can be inferred about the Industrial Revolution from the passage?

**Answer choices:**

A. It immediately improved working conditions for all laborers.
B. It changed how goods were produced and where production took place.
C. It ended the use of hand-made goods entirely.
D. It began in America before spreading to Britain.

**Evidence-based reasoning:**

- **Choice A** is contradicted: the passage states the shift “led to poor working conditions, long hours, and child labor,” and reforms came “over time.”
- **Choice B** is supported: the passage describes the move from hand production in homes to machine production in factories, which is an inference about change in production methods and locations.
- **Choice C** is too extreme: the passage does not say hand-made goods disappeared entirely.
- **Choice D** is contradicted: the passage says the revolution “began in Britain.”

**Correct answer: B**

## Limits and Common Misapplications

The evidence-check method is reliable, but it has boundaries. Be aware of these pitfalls.

### 1. Over-Interpreting Inference Questions

Inference questions allow a small logical step, but you cannot go beyond what the passage supports. If you find yourself adding information not implied by the text, you are over-interpreting. Stick to what is reasonably suggested.

### 2. Choosing the “Most True” Answer Instead of the “Most Supported” Answer

An answer may be factually correct in the real world, but if the passage does not support it, it is wrong for the test. Always prioritize passage evidence over general knowledge.

### 3. Missing Qualifiers

Words like “some,” “often,” “may,” and “likely” can change the meaning of a statement. If an answer drops a qualifier or replaces it with an extreme word, it may be unsupported.

### 4. Falling for Partial Matches

An answer that contains correct words from the passage but attaches them to a different idea is a common trap. Read the entire answer and compare it to the passage as a whole, not just for matching keywords.

### 5. Rushing Through Sentence Insertion or Summary Questions

These question types require careful attention to logical flow and main ideas. Do not apply the evidence-check method mechanically; ensure the inserted sentence fits the paragraph’s structure, or that summary choices capture the passage’s key points without minor details.

### 6. Assuming All Long Answers Are Correct

Length does not indicate correctness. A long answer may contain extra unsupported information. Evaluate each part of the answer against the passage.

## How to Practice This Method

To build fluency with evidence checking:

1. **Use official practice materials** from ETS to familiarize yourself with question styles.
2. **Work through each question slowly**, applying the six-step method.
3. **After answering, verify your choice** by citing the exact passage text.
4. **Review incorrect answers** to understand why they were wrong and what evidence you missed.
5. **Time yourself gradually** as you improve accuracy.

The goal is not speed alone, but accuracy through disciplined evidence evaluation.

## Summary

The TOEFL Reading section tests your ability to find and use evidence from academic texts. By making the decision to choose only the answer directly supported by the passage, and by following a repeatable evidence-check method, you can reduce errors and improve your score. Practice with original examples, learn to eliminate unsupported choices, and avoid common misapplications.
