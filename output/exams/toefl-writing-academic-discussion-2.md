## The Exact Decision Every TOEFL Academic Discussion Writer Must Make

Before you write a single sentence in the TOEFL Writing Academic Discussion task, you face one decision: **which stance will you take on the professor's question, and how will you relate it to the two student responses already posted?**

This is not a multiple-choice question. The test does not tell you which position is "correct." But every high-scoring response makes this decision explicitly and then sustains it throughout the paragraph. The difference between a 3 and a 5 on the rubric often comes down to whether the writer hesitates on this decision mid-paragraph, shifts position, or buries it under summary.

The Academic Discussion task asks you to contribute to an online class forum. A professor poses a question. Two students—Paul and Monica, typically—offer their opinions. You write your own reply. You have about 10 minutes and must produce at least 100 words. The task measures your ability to present and support an opinion in written English.

The decision you make at the top of the page determines everything that follows: what evidence you select, how you structure your paragraph, and whether your response reads as a coherent contribution or a collection of ideas.

## Why This Decision Is Harder Than It Looks

Many test-takers treat the two student responses as a menu and pick whichever opinion is easier to defend. This approach creates three problems:

**Problem 1: Summary bloat.** When you are unsure of your own position, you spend too much time restating what Paul and Monica said. The rubric rewards original contribution, not paraphrase.

**Problem 2: Position drift.** If you start by agreeing with Paul but then introduce a point that actually supports Monica, your paragraph loses coherence. Readers—and raters—notice when the argument pulls in two directions.

**Problem 3: Weak support.** A half-chosen stance produces half-formed reasoning. You might offer a vague example or a claim without explanation, which drops your score on the "development" dimension.

The method below is designed to lock in your decision before you write and keep it locked in while you write.

## A Repeatable Four-Step Method

### Step 1: Identify the Professor's Core Question in One Sentence

Read the prompt and write down the question the professor is asking. Do this mentally or on your scratch paper. It takes 15 seconds.

**Why this works.** The professor's question is the anchor. Every sentence in your response should relate back to it. If you skip this step, you risk answering a slightly different question—especially when the prompt is phrased indirectly or contains multiple clauses.

### Step 2: Read Paul and Monica and Map Their Positions

For each student, note:
- Their stance (agree, disagree, or qualified)
- Their main reason
- Any example or detail they provide

Do not write a summary. Write a one-line label for each.

**Why this works.** You need to know what positions already exist in the discussion so you can place your own contribution strategically. You might agree with one student, agree with both but add a new dimension, or disagree with both and propose an alternative. Each choice is valid—what matters is that you choose deliberately.

### Step 3: Make Your Stance Decision Before You Write the First Sentence

Choose one of these three options:

- **Agree with one student.** State your agreement clearly and add a new reason or example they did not mention.
- **Partially agree with both.** Acknowledge the valid point in each and explain why one is more important or applicable.
- **Take an independent position.** Disagree with both or accept neither fully, and explain your own view with original support.

Write your chosen stance as a single sentence. This is your thesis.

**Why this works.** A pre-written thesis prevents drift. When you know exactly what you are arguing, every subsequent sentence becomes a tool for supporting that argument rather than a wandering observation.

### Step 4: Write One Paragraph That Opens With Your Thesis and Develops It

Your paragraph should follow this structure:

1. **Opening sentence:** Your thesis—your stance on the professor's question, stated directly.
2. **Supporting reason:** One clear reason that backs your thesis.
3. **Example or elaboration:** A specific detail that makes your reason concrete.
4. **Connection back to the prompt:** A sentence that ties your point to the professor's original question.

**Why this works.** The rubric evaluates three dimensions: adequate relevant and well-elaborated support, development of ideas, and language use. This structure guarantees that your response has a clear claim, developed support, and a direct link to the task. It also keeps your response within the typical length needed—100 to 200 words—without padding.

## Original Practice Example 1

**Professor's question:** Our city is considering replacing the public library's physical book collection with digital tablets. What do you think is the best approach, and why?

**Paul:** I think the tablets are the better choice. They take up less space, and students can access hundreds of books from their phones. It is more convenient for people who commute.

**Monica:** I disagree. Physical books help people focus better because there are no notifications or internet distractions. Also, not everyone can afford a tablet, so replacing the books would hurt low-income students.

**Your task:** Write your contribution to the discussion.

### Model Response

I believe the city should keep the physical books while gradually introducing a limited number of tablets for specific courses. Monica raises the most important concern: replacing books entirely would exclude students who cannot afford personal devices, and that creates an equity problem that convenience cannot solve. At the same time, Paul is right that tablets offer access that physical collections cannot match, especially for students who need to carry materials on a bus or train. The solution is not an either-or choice. The library could maintain its full print collection for students who prefer reading on paper and who rely on free public resources, while adding a small fleet of tablets for students who need portable access to current editions or multimedia materials. This way, the library serves both groups without forcing either one to choose between distraction and exclusion.

### Evidence-Based Reasoning

This response earns a strong score because it makes the stance decision immediately and sustains it.

**The decision is explicit.** The opening sentence states a clear position: keep physical books while adding a limited number of tablets. The writer does not hedge or shift. Every sentence after that supports this position.

**The support is developed, not listed.** The writer does not simply say "Monica is right and Paul is right." Instead, the writer explains why Monica's equity concern is more important, acknowledges Paul's point about convenience, and then synthesizes both into a concrete policy recommendation. The example of "a small fleet of tablets for students who need portable access to current editions or multimedia materials" is specific and original—it does not repeat what either student said.

**The connection to the prompt is maintained.** The final sentence ties the recommendation back to the professor's question about "the best approach." The response never drifts into a general discussion of technology in education.

**Common misapplication to avoid.** A weak response to this prompt might say: "Both Paul and Monica have good points. Paul says tablets are convenient. Monica says books help focus. I think we should do both." This response fails because it states a position without developing it. It repeats the students' points without adding original reasoning. It does not explain what "both" means in practice. The difference between this weak response and the model is not vocabulary—it is whether the writer made a real decision and sustained it.

## Original Practice Example 2

**Professor's question:** Some universities require all first-year students to live on campus. Do you think this is a good policy, and why?

**Paul:** Yes, living on campus helps students make friends and join clubs more easily. It also reduces the commute, so students can spend more time studying.

**Monica:** I think it depends on the student. Some students work part-time jobs and need to live off campus to save money. For them, a required on-campus policy would create a financial burden.

**Your task:** Write your contribution to the discussion.

### Model Response

Requiring all first-year students to live on campus is a well-intentioned policy, but it should include exceptions for students who work or have financial constraints. Paul is correct that campus housing supports social integration and reduces commute time, both of which are linked to higher retention rates in the first year. However, Monica's point about financial burden is decisive: a policy that applies equally to all students without accounting for different economic situations will push some students out of college rather than into it. Universities that have tried strict on-campus requirements often see a drop in enrollment from low-income families, even if the average GPA of those who stay rises slightly. A better approach is to make on-campus housing the default option while offering targeted waivers and housing stipends for students who work more than twenty hours a week or who qualify for need-based aid. This preserves the social and academic benefits Paul describes without treating financial hardship as a personal failure.

### Evidence-Based Reasoning

This response demonstrates the method in a more complex scenario where the writer chooses the "partial agreement with both" option.

**The decision is nuanced but unambiguous.** The opening sentence states a qualified position: the policy is well-intentioned but should include exceptions. This is a real decision, not a non-answer. The writer could have simply agreed with Paul or Monica; instead, the writer chose a third path and defended it.

**The support distinguishes between agreement and decisive reasoning.** The writer acknowledges Paul's point about social integration and commute time, then pivots to why Monica's concern is "decisive." This is not summary—it is evaluation. The writer explains the consequence of ignoring Monica's point: students leave college. This is an original claim that neither student made.

**The example is specific and original.** The reference to "a drop in enrollment from low-income families" and "housing stipends for students who work more than twenty hours a week" goes beyond what Paul and Monica said. These details show the writer is developing an argument, not restating others'.

**The connection to the prompt is sustained.** The final sentence returns to the professor's question about whether the policy is "good," reframing it as a question of how to design the policy rather than whether to keep or abandon it.

**Common misapplication to avoid.** A weak response might say: "I agree with both students. Paul says living on campus is good for making friends. Monica says it is bad for poor students. I think universities should listen to both sides." This response fails because it offers no original position. "Listening to both sides" is not a stance on the professor's question. It is a refusal to take one. The rubric requires a clear opinion with support.

## How to Practice This Method

The method works only if you internalize it through repetition. Here is a structured practice routine:

**Drill 1: Decision mapping.** Take any Academic Discussion prompt and spend two minutes completing Steps 1 and 2 only. Write the professor's question in one sentence. Write one-line labels for Paul and Monica. Then write your stance decision as a single sentence. Do not write the full paragraph. This drill isolates the decision-making step, which is the part most test-takers rush.

**Drill 2: Thesis-first writing.** Write ten opening sentences for ten different prompts. Each sentence must state a clear stance without using words like "maybe," "it depends," or "both are right." If your sentence cannot stand alone as a position, rewrite it.

**Drill 3: Full responses under time pressure.** Write three complete responses in 10-minute intervals. After each one, check: (a) Does the first sentence state a clear stance? (b) Does every sentence after that support that stance? (c) Is there at least one original detail that neither student mentioned? If any answer is no, revise.

**Drill 4: Error audit.** Collect your weakest responses and identify which misapplication pattern they match: summary bloat, position drift, or weak support. Target your practice to the pattern that appears most often.

## Limits of This Method

This method is a framework, not a guarantee. It has boundaries you should understand.

**It does not replace language accuracy.** A perfectly structured paragraph with frequent grammar errors will still score low. The rubric evaluates language use as a separate dimension. Practice this method alongside separate grammar and vocabulary work.

**It does not help if you misread the prompt.** If you identify the professor's question incorrectly in Step 1, every sentence after that will address the wrong issue. Read the prompt slowly. If a sentence is ambiguous, re-read it.

**It does not work if you run out of time.** The method assumes you can complete all four steps in the available time. If you consistently run out of time, you need to practice writing faster, not changing the method. Shorten your examples, reduce your sentences, and prioritize the thesis and one developed reason over two underdeveloped ones.

**It does not tell you which stance is "better."** The rubric does not reward a particular opinion. It rewards a clearly stated and well-supported opinion. You can agree with Paul, agree with Monica, or take an independent position. The score depends on how well you develop your choice, not on what your choice is.

## What the Rubric Actually Rewards

ETS publishes a rubric for the Academic Discussion task. Understanding it clarifies why the method works.

The rubric evaluates three dimensions:

1. **Adequate, relevant, and well-elaborated support.** Your response must present a position and develop it with reasons and examples. Summary of the prompt or the other students' responses without original contribution does not count as support.

2. **Development of ideas.** Your ideas must be connected and progress logically. A list of unrelated points is not development. A paragraph that moves from claim to reason to example to connection is.

3. **Language use.** Your grammar, sentence structure, and word choice must be accurate enough to communicate your meaning clearly. Minor errors are acceptable if they do not obscure meaning.

The method above is designed to satisfy all three dimensions simultaneously. Step 3 ensures a clear position (dimension 1). Step 4 ensures logical development (dimension 2). The practice drills improve language accuracy over time (dimension 3).

## The One Mistake That Costs the Most Points

The single most common error is writing a response that answers a different question than the one the professor asked. This happens when test-takers focus so much on Paul and Monica that they lose track of the professor's original prompt. The result is a paragraph that discusses the students' disagreement rather than the issue the professor raised.

To prevent this, keep the professor's question visible while you write. If you are working on scratch paper, write it at the top of the page and refer to it before you finish. If you are working directly in the response box, keep your eyes on the prompt section between sentences.

## Summary: The Decision and the Method

The TOEFL Academic Discussion task asks you to make one decision and sustain it: **what is your stance on the professor's question, and how does it relate to the two student responses?** The four-step method—identify the question, map the students, choose your stance, write with a thesis-first structure—gives you a repeatable process for making that decision and executing it under time pressure.

Practice the method in isolation before combining it with language accuracy work. Drill the decision step until it becomes automatic. When you sit for the test, the method should feel like a routine you have already completed many times, not a new strategy you are trying for the first time.
